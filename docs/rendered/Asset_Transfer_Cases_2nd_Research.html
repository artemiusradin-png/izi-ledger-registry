Asset Transfer Cases: Second Research

Additional Interstate Models for Post-Confiscation Transfer

Author
Affiliation

Artem Radin

Intern

Published

April 7, 2026

1 Purpose

This second note continues the first asset-transfer memo but shifts to new interstate cases not already substantially covered in the project. The focus remains on actual post-confiscation or post-forfeiture transfers, not merely freezing or seizure.

The main question is practical and Ukraine-oriented: which legal structures have allowed one state to transfer criminally confiscated assets to another state or to a monitored public-benefit vehicle, and which of those structures could inform a future Ukraine model?

This draft follows the same logic as the first memo:

  • identify the accusation and the parties;
  • trace the legal step that made transfer possible;
  • identify the legal basis for the return;
  • record how the returned assets were used;
  • assess whether monitoring, conditionality, or a joint project made the transfer politically and legally workable.

2 Working Pattern

The strongest interstate return cases usually have four elements:

  1. a final forfeiture, confiscation, or remission decision in the recovering state;
  2. a second allocation instrument directing the assets to the receiving state or fund;
  3. a public-benefit use restriction rather than an unrestricted treasury payment;
  4. a monitoring architecture strong enough to answer misuse concerns.

For Ukraine, the most useful cases are therefore not simply large returns. They are cases where confiscated value was channeled through a trust fund, monitored project vehicle, independent auditor, civil-society oversight mechanism, or multistate agreement.

5 Priority Case Analysis

5.1 Switzerland -> Uzbekistan (Karimova)

5.1.1 Essence of the accusation

The case concerns Gulnara Karimova, daughter of former Uzbek president Islam Karimov. Swiss materials describe the assets as connected to criminal proceedings in Switzerland involving illicitly acquired assets and official statements tie the matter to corruption around the award of lucrative telecommunications contracts.123

5.1.2 Parties

  • Confiscating state: Switzerland
  • Receiving state: Uzbekistan
  • Implementation vehicle: UN Multi-Partner Trust Fund
  • Beneficiary: population of Uzbekistan

5.1.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. Switzerland definitively confiscated an initial tranche of around USD 131 million in one of the Swiss criminal proceedings.4
  2. On 11 September 2020, Switzerland and Uzbekistan signed a framework agreement setting the principles and stages for restitution.5
  3. On 16 August 2022, they signed the legally binding restitution agreement after negotiations over governance and implementation.6
  4. The assets were to be returned through a new UN Multi-Partner Trust Fund, not as a direct transfer into the Uzbek state budget.78

5.1.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Confiscation Definitive confiscation in Swiss criminal proceedings
Transfer abroad 2020 framework agreement followed by 2022 restitution agreement
Restrictions on use UN trust-fund architecture; SDG-oriented public-benefit projects; governance rules and consultative role for civil society

5.1.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

Swiss and UN materials show the funds were designed to support the population of Uzbekistan through projects aligned with the UN Sustainable Development Cooperation Framework. Swiss follow-up reporting links the mechanism to health-sector implementation, including upgrades to maternity and neonatal care units and training of healthcare workers.91011

5.1.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

This is one of the strongest current models for Ukraine because it shows how a confiscating state can avoid the objection that funds should not be wired directly into a politically sensitive treasury account. The assets are instead routed through a neutral international vehicle, with joint governance and public-benefit restrictions.

5.1.7 Verification note

  • Transfer mechanism verified: Yes
  • Restitution agreement verified: Yes
  • Use-of-funds track verified: Yes
  • Later amounts: after the 2020 framework (~USD 131 million initially identified), Swiss official reporting through 2025 described larger cumulative confiscated totals being restituted through the same UN fund architecture.12
  • Judicial source gap: public Swiss court texts are less visible than the executive restitution materials

5.2 U.S. / Jersey -> Nigeria (Abacha, 2020 tranche)

5.2.1 Essence of the accusation

The U.S. and Jersey materials treat the funds as proceeds of the kleptocracy of General Sani Abacha and his co-conspirators. The allegations included embezzlement, misappropriation, extortion, and laundering of billions from Nigeria through the international financial system.1819

5.2.2 Parties

  • Forfeiting state: United States
  • Enforcing jurisdiction for located assets: Jersey
  • Recipient: Federal Republic of Nigeria

5.2.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. In 2014, Judge John D. Bates in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia entered judgment forfeiting roughly USD 480 million in assets tied to the case (DOJ described on the order of half a billion dollars in related accounts worldwide).20
  2. After U.S. appeals were exhausted in 2018, Jersey enforced the U.S. judgment against more than USD 308 million located there.21
  3. On 3 February 2020, the U.S., Nigeria, and Jersey executed a trilateral repatriation agreement.22
  4. On 4 May 2020, DOJ announced transfer of USD 311,797,876.11 to Nigeria pursuant to that agreement.23

5.2.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Forfeiture 2014 U.S. civil forfeiture judgment
Cross-border enforcement Jersey enforcement of the U.S. judgment
Transfer abroad 2020 trilateral repatriation agreement

5.2.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

DOJ’s 2020 transfer release confirms the transfer followed the trilateral agreement and emphasizes the presence of lawful process and transparency and accountability safeguards.2425 The case is especially useful when read together with later Abacha repatriation agreements, which made the infrastructure uses and monitoring architecture more explicit.

5.2.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

The practical lesson is not just “money was returned.” It is that one state’s forfeiture judgment can be enforced in another allied jurisdiction and then repatriated through a structured agreement. That is directly relevant if Ukrainian-benefit assets are scattered across multiple partner states.

5.2.7 Verification note

  • Forfeiture judgment basis verified: Yes
  • Trilateral return instrument verified: Yes
  • Exact project-level use in this tranche: less explicit in the DOJ summary than in later Nigeria return agreements

5.2.8 Sources

  • DOJ trilateral agreement, 3 Feb 202026
  • DOJ transfer announcement, 4 May 202027

5.3 U.S. / UK -> Nigeria (Abacha, 2022 tranche)

5.3.1 Essence of the accusation

This is a second Abacha repatriation track involving the same larger kleptocracy allegations, but with additional assets located in the United Kingdom and recovered through cooperation in restraint and enforcement.2829

5.3.2 Parties

  • Forfeiting state: United States
  • Supporting enforcement jurisdiction: United Kingdom
  • Recipient: Federal Republic of Nigeria
  • Project administrator: Nigeria Sovereign Investment Authority (NSIA)

5.3.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. DOJ relied on the same 2014 D.D.C. forfeiture judgment that had already produced the 2020 Jersey tranche.30
  2. The UK enforced the U.S. judgment against additional assets and cooperated in investigation and restraint.31
  3. On 23 August 2022, the U.S. and Nigeria signed an agreement to repatriate over USD 23 million.32
  4. On 17 November 2022, DOJ announced the transfer of over USD 20.6 million in accordance with that agreement.33

5.3.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Forfeiture 2014 U.S. civil forfeiture judgment
Cross-border enforcement UK enforcement of U.S. judgment against assets located in the UK
Transfer abroad 2022 U.S.-Nigeria repatriation agreement

5.3.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

The agreement directed the funds to three already authorized public infrastructure projects: the Second Niger Bridge, Lagos-Ibadan Expressway, and Abuja-Kano road. DOJ expressly noted administration by NSIA, review by an independent auditor, monitoring by an independent civil society organization, and a bar on use to benefit alleged perpetrators or pay contingency-fee lawyers.34

5.3.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

This is a strong case for a Ukraine model because it shows how partner governments can convert a general “return the money” decision into a project-specific public-benefit arrangement with named projects, institutional administration, audit, and exclusions.

5.3.7 Verification note

  • Agreement verified: Yes
  • Transfer verified: Yes
  • Use-of-funds restrictions verified: Yes

  • DOJ transfer, 17 Nov 202236

5.4 U.S. -> Nigeria (Alison-Madueke / Aluko-Omokore)

5.4.1 Essence of the accusation

According to DOJ, Nigerian businessmen Kolawole Aluko and Olajide Omokore conspired with others to pay bribes to former Nigerian Minister for Petroleum Resources Diezani Alison-Madueke from 2011 to 2015. In return, Alison-Madueke allegedly steered lucrative oil contracts to companies linked to them. DOJ states that more than USD 100 million in proceeds was laundered into and through the United States and used to purchase luxury real estate and the yacht Galactica Star.37

5.4.2 Parties

  • Forfeiting state: United States
  • Recipient state: Nigeria
  • Implementation vehicles: World Bank trust fund and International Institute for Justice and the Rule of Law (IIJ)

5.4.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. U.S. authorities pursued forfeiture of assets tied to corruption, bribery, and money laundering involving the Nigerian oil sector.38
  2. Nigeria provided significant evidentiary and investigative assistance to the United States.39
  3. On 10 January 2025, the U.S. entered into an agreement with Nigeria to transfer approximately USD 52.88 million in forfeited proceeds.40

5.4.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Forfeiture U.S. forfeiture proceedings arising from corruption and laundering
Transfer abroad 2025 U.S.-Nigeria transfer agreement
Authority to share DOJ notes Attorney General may share successful forfeiture proceeds with foreign countries that participated in seizure or forfeiture, in coordination with Treasury and State

5.4.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

The agreement is unusually useful because it split the funds into two distinct public-benefit tracks:

  • USD 50 million for the Distributed Access Through Renewable Energy Scale-Up project, through a World Bank trust fund and Rural Electrification Agency implementation;
  • about USD 2.88 million for Nigeria’s contribution to the International Institute for Justice and the Rule of Law, supporting regional criminal-justice and counter-terrorism capacity-building.41

The agreement also included World Bank oversight controls, auditing, publication requirements, periodic progress reporting, and public reporting.42

5.4.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

This is one of the best current analogues for Ukraine because it demonstrates a split-beneficiary architecture: part of the return can go to infrastructure or recovery spending and part can reinforce rule-of-law or security institutions. That is closer to Ukraine’s likely needs than a single unrestricted transfer.

5.5 Jersey -> Kenya (Windward / Gichuru-Okemo)

5.5.1 Essence of the accusation

The Jersey proceedings concerned Windward Trading Limited, whose beneficial owner was Samuel Gichuru, then chief executive of Kenya Power and Lighting Company. Jersey states the company admitted laundering proceeds of corruption linked to valuable Kenyan contracts, with the case also involving former Kenyan Energy Minister Chrysanthus Okemo in connected extradition efforts.444546

5.5.2 Parties

  • Confiscating jurisdiction: Jersey
  • Recipient: Kenya
  • Framework partners: Jersey, Kenya, United Kingdom, Switzerland under FRACCK

5.5.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. On 24 February 2016, Windward pleaded guilty before the Royal Court of Jersey and the court imposed a confiscation order of GBP 3,281,897.40 and USD 540,330.69.47
  2. On 3 March 2017, Jersey and Kenya signed an agreement paving the way for return of over GBP 3 million.48
  3. In December 2018, Jersey signed the Framework for Return of Assets from Corruption and Crime to Kenya (FRACCK), a multilateral framework also involving the UK and Switzerland.49
  4. On 28 March 2022, Jersey and Kenya signed an Asset Recovery Agreement enabling the first return under FRACCK, with GBP 3 million to support Kenya’s response to COVID-19.50

5.5.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Confiscation Royal Court confiscation order after criminal conviction
Transfer abroad 2017 bilateral return step; FRACCK framework; 2022 Asset Recovery Agreement
Restrictions on use Development and public-benefit objectives under FRACCK; Kenya-defined projects agreed with partners

5.5.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

The 2022 Jersey statement says the return would support Kenya’s COVID-19 response, while the FRACCK framework required returned assets to be used for clearly identified development objectives that benefit the Kenyan people and support Vision 2030.5152

5.5.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

This is important because it shows how a return framework can exist before a particular transfer is finalized. For Ukraine, a standing framework among partner states could reduce the need to renegotiate principles in each case.

5.5.7 Verification note

  • Confiscation verified: Yes
  • Framework agreement verified: Yes
  • First return under framework verified: Yes

5.6 Jersey -> Mozambique (Fragoso)

5.6.1 Essence of the accusation

Jersey’s 2024 statement explains that the returned funds were linked to Carlos Fragoso, a Mozambican official who had held senior roads and bridge positions. The funds were held in a Jersey trust and treated as tainted property derived from bribes paid to secure construction contracts in Mozambique.57

5.6.2 Parties

  • Confiscating jurisdiction: Jersey
  • Recipient: Republic of Mozambique

5.6.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. Jersey had previously litigated related trust issues and identified earlier deceptive source-of-income representations.58
  2. Following cooperation from Mozambican authorities, Jersey’s Economic Crime and Confiscation Unit used the Forfeiture of Assets (Civil Proceedings) (Jersey) Law 2018 to obtain forfeiture of funds held in the Tolvex Trust.59
  3. On 7 May 2024, Jersey and Mozambique signed a Memorandum of Understanding returning over GBP 829,500.60

5.6.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Forfeiture Jersey civil forfeiture under the 2018 law
Transfer abroad 2024 Jersey-Mozambique Memorandum of Understanding
Restrictions on use Funds earmarked for financial-crime and asset-recovery institutions and training

5.6.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

The MoU earmarked most of the funds to support Mozambique’s Central Office for Asset Recovery, Central Office for Combatting Corruption, Asset Management Office, Attorney General’s Office, and National Criminal Investigation Service. The package also funded a five-day training course on international and judicial cooperation in criminal matters for trainees from all eleven provinces.61

5.6.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

This case is smaller than the others, but it is analytically strong because it treats returned assets as a tool to build state recovery capacity itself. That is useful if some Ukraine-bound returns are directed to anti-corruption, asset-management, or prosecutorial capacity rather than only reconstruction projects.

5.7 U.S. -> Kyrgyz Republic (Bakiyev)

5.7.1 Essence of the accusation

DOJ tied the returned funds to corruption and theft of government funds by the prior regime of Kurmanbek Bakiyev and his son Maxim Bakiyev. The assets were identified in the United States through a criminal prosecution of Eugene Gourevitch for wire fraud and insider trading, after which a USD 6 million forfeiture order was entered.63

5.7.2 Parties

  • Forfeiting state: United States
  • Recipient: Government of the Kyrgyz Republic
  • Claim vehicle: Petition for remission filed by Kyrgyz authorities

5.7.3 Procedural path and operative decisions

  1. In the Eastern District of New York prosecution of Eugene Gourevitch, the court entered a USD 6 million forfeiture order.64
  2. The Kyrgyz government filed a petition for remission with DOJ MLARS claiming the forfeited funds traced back to monies stolen by Maxim Bakiyev from Kyrgyz state authorities and other banking institutions.65
  3. DOJ granted the remission petition on 4 October 2018.66
  4. On 26 February 2019, DOJ announced repatriation of approximately USD 4.6 million collected so far, with additional efforts to locate the remainder.67

5.7.4 Basis for return

Element Legal basis
Forfeiture U.S. criminal forfeiture order
Transfer abroad DOJ remission process following foreign-state petition
Restrictions on use Jointly announced public-benefit uses focused on social and anti-corruption projects

5.7.5 Use of returned assets / joint projects

DOJ and the Kyrgyz government stated the funds would be used for:

  • healthcare access in rural areas through medical equipment;
  • water-supply facilities and drinking-water systems;
  • anti-corruption institutions and transparency of court proceedings.68

5.7.6 Why it matters for Ukraine

This case is important because it shows a different route from bilateral restitution agreements: a foreign state petitioning for remission out of a U.S. forfeiture case. It may not be the central Ukraine model, but it shows another legal mechanism for directing forfeited value back to a harmed state.

6 Preliminary Comparative Lessons For Ukraine

6.1 1. Confiscation is usually necessary but not sufficient

In almost all useful cases, the judicial forfeiture or confiscation step did not itself settle destination and use. A second step was required: a bilateral agreement, a trilateral agreement, a trust-fund instrument, or a remission decision.

6.2 2. The politically safest route is not a simple treasury transfer

The most transferable models for Ukraine are the ones that route funds through:

  • a UN trust fund;
  • a World Bank trust fund;
  • a state project vehicle with external audit;
  • a framework agreement with predefined safeguards.

That pattern appears in the Uzbekistan, Nigeria-2022, and Nigeria-2025 cases.

6.3 3. Monitoring architecture is not cosmetic

Monitoring is often what makes return politically acceptable. The recurring mechanisms are:

  • independent audit;
  • public reporting;
  • external implementation controls;
  • civil-society monitoring;
  • project earmarking;
  • prohibition on benefit to perpetrators or related persons.

Ukraine-facing work should therefore treat monitoring as part of the legal design, not an afterthought.

6.4 4. Multi-state enforcement matters

The Abacha 2020 and 2022 tranches are especially relevant because they show how a forfeiture judgment from one state can be enforced against assets located in another jurisdiction and only then transferred onward. That is likely to matter in any serious Ukraine asset-recovery pipeline.

6.5 5. A future Ukraine model may need more than one destination channel

The January 10, 2025 U.S.-Nigeria agreement is particularly instructive because it split the assets between:

  • a recovery-style public project channel; and
  • a rule-of-law / security-capacity channel.

That may be a better template for Ukraine than a one-bucket structure.

7 Tentative Conclusion

At this stage, the strongest new cases for a Ukraine-oriented final note are:

  1. Switzerland -> Uzbekistan (Karimova) for the UN trust-fund model;
  2. U.S. -> Nigeria (Alison-Madueke / Aluko-Omokore) for a split architecture combining infrastructure-type spending and institutional capacity-building;
  3. U.S. / UK -> Nigeria (Abacha, 2022 tranche) for project-specific safeguards after cross-border enforcement;
  4. Jersey -> Kenya (Windward / FRACCK) for a standing framework model;
  5. U.S. -> Kyrgyz Republic (Bakiyev) for the remission route;
  6. Jersey -> Mozambique (Fragoso) for anti-corruption institution-building as an end use.

The emerging lesson is that a plausible Ukraine model will likely require:

  • finalized confiscation in the holding state;
  • a separate interstate allocation instrument;
  • a protected beneficiary vehicle;
  • strong monitoring and public reporting;
  • and, in some cases, a split between reconstruction uses and institutional rule-of-law uses.
