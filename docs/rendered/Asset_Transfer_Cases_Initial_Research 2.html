Asset Transfer Cases: Initial Research

Verified Domestic and Cross-Border Transfers of Confiscated Criminal Assets

Author
Affiliation

Artem Radin

Intern

Published

March 4, 2026

1 Purpose

This note is an initial case-based research draft responding to the request to focus on actual transfers of confiscated criminal assets, not just on abstract legal pathways. It identifies verified examples of:

  • domestic transfers from forfeited assets to victims; and
  • cross-border transfers from one state to another or to a monitored public-benefit mechanism.

The note relies primarily on official sources. Court decisions and formal instruments are included where they were readily identifiable from official materials.

2 Preliminary Finding

The first review suggests a clear pattern. In the strongest public examples, asset transfer usually requires three distinct steps:

  1. Final confiscation / forfeiture / settlement in the recovering state.
  2. A legal allocation mechanism for victims or a foreign beneficiary.
  3. A monitored implementation vehicle, especially in cross-border corruption cases.

This is why the most developed models are not simple “state A seizes, then wires money to state B” cases. They usually combine confiscation with a remission process, a restitution agreement, or a monitored trust-fund or project structure.

4 Court Decision Links and Transfer Nexus (Priority Cases)

4.1 Switzerland -> Nigeria (Abacha)

  • Court decision: Swiss Federal Supreme Court, ATF 131 II 169 (case 1A.215/2004, 7 Feb 2005).8
  • Short summary: The Court addressed mutual legal assistance and asset return conditions in the Abacha matter, including transfer of funds under Swiss international cooperation law (IRSG) where criminal proceedings were ongoing in the requesting state.
  • How this enabled transfer: The decision helped validate the Swiss legal path for releasing Abacha-linked assets under judicial cooperation rules. Later confiscation actions and the 2017 Switzerland-Nigeria-World Bank agreement then operationalized the actual monitored transfer.

4.2 U.S. -> Malaysia (1MDB)

  • Court decision: U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, United States v. PetroSaudi Oil Services (Venezuela) Ltd., Nos. 21-56228 and 22-55025, filed 13 Jun 2023.9
  • Short summary: The Ninth Circuit affirmed district-court orders preserving and allowing seizure of assets in a civil forfeiture action tied to alleged 1MDB proceeds (including a USD 380 million arbitration fund).
  • How this enabled transfer: By upholding protective/seizure authority over the res, the decision supported asset preservation and forfeiture progression, which is the legal precondition for later DOJ repatriation decisions such as the 2024 transfer tranche to Malaysia.

4.3 UK -> Nigeria (Ibori)

  • Court-linked source currently available: UK-Nigeria return annex confirming that returned funds were recovered through UK confiscation orders made since 2010.10
  • Short summary: The annex is not itself a judicial decision but it is the key official legal instrument identifying the confiscation-order basis for the transfer.
  • How this enabled transfer: The confiscation orders created recoverable value under UK law; the 2021 bilateral return framework then directed that value to specific monitored projects in Nigeria.
  • Verification caveat: A full-text public copy of the underlying Southwark confiscation orders has not yet been located in open official databases.

6 Domestic Transfer Cases

6.1 1. U.S. Madoff Victim Fund

This is one of the clearest public examples of forfeited assets being redistributed to victims at scale. On 30 December 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice announced the 10th and final distribution of the Madoff Victim Fund. The Department stated that more than USD 4.3 billion in forfeited funds had been paid to 40,930 victims in 127 countries, bringing aggregate recovery to 93.71% of fraud losses.13

The legal step preceding the transfer was the U.S. government’s recovery of assets through civil and criminal forfeiture connected to the Bernard L. Madoff fraud scheme. The transfer mechanism was not an ad hoc political decision, but a formal DOJ-administered victim compensation structure.

Why it matters: this is a strong example of a domestic victim-compensation model built on forfeited assets rather than on ordinary restitution alone.

6.2 2. U.S. Western Union Remission Fund

Western Union is another strong domestic model because the DOJ publicly explains both the forfeiture event and the distribution process. On 9 September 2024, DOJ announced that the Western Union Remission Fund had begun its second distribution in phase two, transferring approximately USD 18.5 million to around 3,000 victims. DOJ stated that, across both phases, the fund had already distributed over USD 420 million to more than 175,000 victims, who received full compensation for their losses.14

The official materials connect the transfer to funds forfeited to the United States from Western Union. The compensation mechanism was remission, a formal administrative process through which forfeited funds can be returned to victims.

Why it matters: this case shows that a transfer to victims can be structured even when the proceeds first pass through the state, provided there is a clear remission framework and a verified class of victims.

7 Cross-Border Transfer Cases

7.1 3. Switzerland -> Nigeria (Abacha II)

Switzerland’s return of Abacha assets is one of the best documented cross-border models. According to the Swiss Federal Department of Foreign Affairs, on 4 December 2017 Switzerland, Nigeria and the World Bank signed a tripartite agreement governing the restitution of approximately USD 321 million to Nigeria’s population.15

The official Swiss explanation is important because it identifies the legal step that came before transfer: the funds had been repatriated and confiscated by Switzerland as part of criminal proceedings brought by the public prosecutor’s office of Geneva against Abba Abacha.16 A prior Swiss press release from 8 March 2016 states that the return process became possible after the Attorney General of the Canton of Geneva confiscated the assets.17

The agreement did not authorize an unrestricted transfer to the Nigerian state budget. Instead, it required World Bank-supported monitoring, tranche-based disbursement, and anti-misuse safeguards. The World Bank’s own factsheet likewise describes the arrangement as a monitored repatriation process.18

Why it matters: this is a leading example of confiscation plus monitored interstate restitution, which is likely more relevant for Ukraine than an unconditioned bilateral transfer.

7.2 4. UK -> Nigeria (Ibori)

On 9 March 2021, the UK and Nigeria agreed the return of GBP 4.2 million recovered from associates of former Delta State Governor James Ibori.19 The official UK press release says the money would be used for Nigerian infrastructure and public-benefit projects, and that implementation would be subject to monitoring and audit.

The associated UK publication of the agreement’s annex is useful because it identifies the legal precursor to the transfer. It states that the returned funds were recovered in proceedings resulting in confiscation orders made since 2010.20 It also restricts the use of the money to specified projects and bars disbursement to alleged perpetrators and related persons.

Why it matters: this is a concise example of confiscation orders plus a bilateral implementation agreement. The transfer did not follow automatically from confiscation; the bilateral arrangement operationalized the destination and controls.

Court-link status: this initial tranche confirms the confiscation-order basis from official UK materials, but a full-text public copy of the operative confiscation orders is still pending.

7.3 5. U.S. -> Malaysia (1MDB)

The U.S. Department of Justice provides unusually clear official reporting on the repatriation of 1MDB proceeds. On 13 June 2024, DOJ announced an additional USD 156 million repatriation to Malaysia, bringing the total returned by DOJ to approximately USD 1.4 billion.21

DOJ links these returns to the long-running U.S. forfeiture campaign involving 1MDB proceeds. The Department states that, since July 2016, it had filed 41 civil forfeiture actions and seized more than USD 1.7 billion in assets allegedly traceable to the scheme.22

A published judicial layer is available in a related forfeiture track: in United States v. PetroSaudi Oil Services (Venezuela) Ltd. (Ninth Circuit, 13 June 2023), the court affirmed interlocutory orders preserving and permitting seizure of assets allegedly connected to 1MDB proceeds.23 This did not itself order repatriation to Malaysia, but it upheld legal control over assets in the forfeiture pipeline that is necessary before transfer decisions.

Why it matters: 1MDB shows that large cross-border returns are possible, but only after a lengthy forfeiture phase, substantial tracing work, and a dedicated repatriation decision by the recovering state.

8 Switzerland as a Leading Model

Management specifically mentioned Switzerland, and the official materials strongly support that focus.

The Swiss Federal Department of Foreign Affairs states that Switzerland has returned more than USD 2 billion to countries of origin pursuant to restitution agreements.24 The State Secretariat for International Finance similarly states that, over roughly three decades, Switzerland has returned more than USD 2 billion for the benefit of affected populations in countries including Nigeria, the Philippines, Peru, Angola and Kazakhstan.25

Swiss official materials also show that Switzerland does not rely on a single model. At least three variants appear in practice:

  • Direct restitution linked to prior confiscation, but governed by a specific agreement.
  • Tripartite arrangements with international organizations, especially the World Bank.
  • Return through a trust fund or special vehicle designed to benefit the population rather than the political elite.

Two additional Swiss cases are especially relevant:

8.1 Switzerland -> Kazakhstan

In 2012, Switzerland and the World Bank signed an agreement to return USD 48 million in assets to Kazakhstan for public-benefit projects.26 The Swiss press release states that the funds had been confiscated in 2011 in criminal proceedings initiated by Geneva judicial authorities on suspicion of money laundering.27

Switzerland’s later official retrospective on Kazakhstan states that an earlier USD 115 million restitution had been completed through the BOTA Kazakh Child and Youth Development Foundation, established by Switzerland, the United States and Kazakhstan in 2007.28

8.2 Switzerland -> Peru

In December 2020, Switzerland signed a trilateral agreement with Peru and Luxembourg to return approximately USD 16.3 million from Switzerland and around EUR 9.7 million from Luxembourg for projects strengthening the rule of law and anti-corruption capacity in Peru.29 Swiss materials explain that these funds could be returned after further confiscation procedures had recently been concluded.30

9 Preliminary Implications for Ukraine

The initial cases point to four practical lessons.

9.1 1. Final confiscation is necessary, but not sufficient

In the cross-border cases reviewed, confiscation or forfeiture was the legal trigger, but another instrument was usually needed before the money could be transferred to the ultimate beneficiary.

9.2 2. Monitoring architecture is central

The Swiss and UK examples show that where the receiving state raises governance concerns, the returning state often insists on:

  • project earmarking;
  • tranche-based release;
  • independent monitoring;
  • civil-society or international-organization oversight.

9.3 3. Switzerland is useful not just because of volume, but because of design

The official Swiss materials are especially valuable because they explain the institutional logic of asset return: restitution agreements, anti-corruption safeguards, and use of the returned funds for the benefit of the population rather than the state elite.

9.4 4. Germany remains important, but public transfer cases are harder to document

Germany has a statutory victim-compensation route after confiscation under StPO §§ 459h-459m.3132 However, publicly accessible, well-documented completed transfer cases are less visible than in the U.S., UK or Swiss materials. For the next research tranche, Germany should be investigated through:

  • published enforcement decisions;
  • prosecutor notices to injured parties;
  • court decisions on objections under the confiscation enforcement provisions; and
  • large public cases where injured state entities sought distribution from confiscated proceeds.

11 Conclusion

The initial research confirms that the strongest precedents for asset transfer are case-specific and mechanism-specific. Domestic transfers are most clearly documented in the U.S. through forfeiture-based victim compensation. Cross-border transfers are most clearly documented in Switzerland, the UK, and the U.S. where confiscation is followed by a separate restitution or repatriation mechanism.

For Ukraine-oriented work, the Swiss and UK models are especially important because they show how confiscated assets can be redirected to public benefit without relying on an unrestricted budget transfer. The most realistic analogue is therefore not simple seizure-to-transfer, but final confiscation plus a monitored allocation agreement.
